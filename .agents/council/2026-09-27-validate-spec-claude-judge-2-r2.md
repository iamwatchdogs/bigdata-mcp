## Narrative

### 1. Restating R1, anchoring to my own reasoning

**WARN / HIGH.** The highest-consequence claims in the document — every one of §11.3 items 2–6 on `-stat`, the exit-code contract, the `none`/`inf` sentinels and `REM_QUOTA` header, the YARN state enum, the Solr parameter-precedence rule, and the Solr 10 `blockUnknown=false` open-cluster hazard — are all confirmed TRUE against `rel/release-3.4.3` source and Apache's own erratum. What holds it below PASS is narrow and specific: §12's "only the ACTIVE RM serves the full API" is contradicted by the RM HA guide (a standby **redirects**), §12's `progress` is a **string** where the REST doc and `AppInfo` both say float, and the completeness gate's field-level truth table / reason-code requirement is genuinely absent.

### 2. Devil's advocate

I wrote this at full strength in the JSON. The compressed version: **the risk is entirely in §11.3, and §11.3 is perfect.** Sixteen hazards read off the Java, including all five that produce a silent wrong answer. §11.1's design decision is *stronger* than the hazards require — rejecting `%` before argv construction makes the worst bug class structurally impossible. On security-posture facts the document is right about everything that matters, and Solr's `blockUnknown` erratum is a confirmation you cannot manufacture. Three judges, three external domains, **zero criticals**. And every R1 finding of mine is a wrong *sentence*, not a wrong *behaviour*, in a read-only single-user tool where the blast radius of a bad parse is a chat window — not a corrupted pipeline, not a leaked key.

That is a real argument. It fails on three specific things, not one general thing — developed in `stress_test_conclusion`.

### 3. The blind spot, stated once

**All three judges partitioned the review by source domain, not by section.** That is why external factual errors were caught beautifully and why ~40% of the document — the parts that assert things about *itself* — went unread.

The two criticals are the sharpest illustration, because they have the same shape: a guarantee stated in one section and silently broken by another.

- **§11.1 says host-key verification "fails closed." §16 turns an omitted `known_hosts` key into asyncssh's fail-open value.** asyncssh's docs are explicit that `known_hosts=None` *disables* checking; and in a TOML-to-Python binding, absent means `None`. The unset sentinel and the disable sentinel are the same value. (§11.1: `https://asyncssh.readthedocs.io/en/latest/`)
- **§3 says use `truststore` for the internal CA. §15.2 never says which client performs the OIDC calls** — and `mcp` 2.2.0 pulls `httpx2>=2.5.0` while §4 names `httpx` and §19 *rejects* httpx entirely.

And the one number nobody checked:

> ```java
> public static final int DEFAULT_RM_MAX_COMPLETED_APPLICATIONS = 1000;
> ```

`rel/release-3.4.3` `YarnConfiguration.java`. §9.1 says 10,000 and calls the ETA sample "adequate." It is wrong by 10×, in the pessimistic direction, and it is the *only* justification offered for trusting a derived number. The property has no `<value>` in `yarn-default.xml` — the default lives in the Java constant, which is exactly why it is easy to get wrong.

### 4. Verdict

**WARN / HIGH — confirmed. Basis changed, verdict did not.** Same verdict as all three of us in R1, reached again by a different route. The research should not be rewritten. The acceptance bar for ship-ready is now: fix the two criticals, fix §9.1's number and give ETA a refusal rule, close the capability enum and make cross-adapter dependencies expressible, rewrite §17.1, reconcile the cap against the ceiling, and give §20 a `check` column.

**17 new findings** — 2 critical, 8 significant, 7 minor. R1's 18 findings stand; none is retracted, one (f-j2-011) is extended in scope, and Judge 3's `known_hosts` finding is reclassified and sharpened rather than repeated.
